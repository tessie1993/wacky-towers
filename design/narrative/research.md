# Story research: how cosy puzzle games carry a story with almost no text

Author: studio narrative content lead. Date: 2026-10-14. Feeds: `design/narrative/campaign-story.md`.
Scope: craft only. We study how these games work; we copy no names, characters, layouts or IP.

## 1. Method and honest limits

- Searched the web (WebSearch, standard mode) for wordless storytelling, chapter-per-world structure,
  storybook framing and light running villains. Findings below come from search-result summaries of
  the pages cited, not from reading each page in full. Most are reviews, press features and wikis,
  so they are **secondary sources**. Treat each as a pointer to craft, not as developer intent.
- **Not sourced:** the searches returned nothing on Tearaway, nothing on A Monster's Expedition, and
  nothing on a per-chapter villain structure for Captain Toad. We draw no rule from those.
- Project context read: `design/art/art-bible.md` (cast, tone, section 5e story-beat composition),
  `assets/art/reference/index.md`, boards `style/01_story_flow.jpg`, `02_story_beats.jpg`,
  `07_story_tutorial_beats.jpg` (layout and mood only, no Nintendo IP),
  `design/levels/plans/campaign-vision.md` (exists; its section 6 is our brief),
  `campaign-redo-plan.md`, `progression.md`, `minigames.md`, `level-identity-spec.md`, `carnival.md`,
  `design/levels/tumble-fair.md`, `design/decisions/2026-10-14-story-cast-decisions.md`.
- Biome mechanics: taken from `progression.md` (biome-by-biome table) and the level names in
  `design/levels/plans/<biome>.md`. The long per-biome docs `design/levels/<biome>.md` were only
  sampled by search, not read end to end. Phase 3 owns mechanics, so this is enough for story hooks.

## 3. Takeaways as rules for the Wacky Towers story

**R1. The stack is the story verb.** Every level's reason to stack must be a story act (mend a bridge,
shore up a dam, raise a lookout). The win changes the scene. (Unpacking, Gris, Monument Valley.)

**R2. One absurd sentence is the premise.** A child must retell it in two sentences, and the premise must
justify the core verb. (Katamari.) Ours: a sky-tower toppled, twelve Keystones flew, you stack them home.

**R3. Props, not paragraphs.** One telling object per beat (a spoon from the Wizard's hat, a tin tortoise
clock). Detail rewards the eye and costs no text. (Unpacking.)

**R4. A running gag carries the through-line.** Katamari's King and Epic Yarn's narrator give a voice to
the joke. We have no voiced narrator and no dialogue in play, so our gag is **visual**: the Wizard's Hat
Dip, a wrong object from his hat in every chapter, paid off once at the finale.

**R5. Text lives between levels.** Storybook panel, ribbon caption of 12 words or fewer, then silence in
play. A skimmer who reads only the 12 captions plus level names still gets the arc. (Monument Valley, Epic
Yarn; art-bible 5e for panel composition.)

**R6. The player is the silent Builder.** Faceless and wordless; the cast reacts to what you build.
(Monument Valley's Ida.) Heroes speak through poses, never lines over the board.

**R7. Music and light mark chapters.** Each recovered Keystone adds one note to a lullaby, and time of day
and palette travel with the arc. (Gris.) Handed to audio-director and art-director as input, not decisions.

**R8. A limit becomes the fantasy.** The player cannot swing a sword; they only place blocks, and the
world holds because of it. (Captain Toad's no-jump limit.)

**R9. Bosses are a rule of three.** Each tier-10 boss has three phases that change the arena, and is beaten
by building. (Captain Toad's repeated boss pattern, plus campaign-vision U9.)

**R10. The villain is the gag, not the menace.** Cause the mess with a charming, misunderstood cause
(Katamari's tipsy King, Toad's snatching bird). Ours: a lonely baby cloud and a forgetful wizard. Nobody
dies; peril is comic. (Vision tone rules.)

**R11. Each level is a compact diorama.** One place, one problem, one set piece, readable at a glance and
frameable. (Captain Toad, Monument Valley, art-bible pillar 3.)

**R12. Reuse is a payoff.** A late, last-chapter return of earlier ideas (Katamari sequel's callbacks) turns
learning into triumph. Our Carnival re-plays every biome idea as a fair game.

**R13. Every level is a minigame with a verb.** From the reference studies (`reference-study-defence.md`, `reference-study-mockups.md`) and the party rules: each level
is one scenario pattern (build a span, race a rising hazard, hold the wall, plug the breach, shelter a guest, carry cargo, outrun a rolling hazard) on 1 or 2 mechanics, with a
themed meter and a visible actor. We tag every beat with one of eight verbs (race, defend, build, destroy, survive, rescue, balance, carry).

**R14. The board is a place with its own silhouette.** The boards are never a floating grid: deck, ledge, ring, bridge span, stair, island chain, hull, crescent. Each level
names its footprint and no two share one inside a biome. Surprise endings come from the studies' non-obvious twists (an empty nest, a door in the rubble, a thirteenth slot
that is a nest box), with at most two "the scary thing was friendly" endings in the whole campaign.

## 4. What we reject

- A dialogue-heavy cutscene layer, a voiced narrator, or any caption over the board.
- Fetch-an-item errands: every chapter's problem is a stacking problem with its own villain and ally.
- Cruel antagonists, deaths, sarcasm, or fourth-wall nagging (vision section 4).
- Any Nintendo character, layout or name; any "Tetris" naming; any reuse of the board logos.
- Carrying over the old story (story-v2, panels S01 to S20): the user chose a fresh story that keeps only
  the cast.
